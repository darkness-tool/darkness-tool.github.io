It's amazing. Why can't people in cold areas eat chili to cool? Break your winter food myth!

Author: Arrested Date of issue: 18 September 2026 Update: 18 September 2026 Reading time: About 7 minutes

When the winter comes, many people feel like they're eating a hot hot hot pot to "swept and cool." However, this seemingly natural idea of survival is a big taboo in a truly cold or a very high climate. Why do people not rely on chili for warmth in a cold climate? It's really hidden in the strict human physiology and science behind it.

For a long time, it has been widely recognized that eating chili makes people hot and sweaty and thus has an excellent chilling effect. Every time the temperature cools down, the spicy foods of Kawasu and others are the first choice. But scientific research points out that this "hot" is just a neuro-system illusion, not a real increase in core body temperature.

How did pepperoni fool your brain?

The hotness of chili is due to the fact that the paprika content directly stimulates thermal receptors on the skin and mucous membranes. When these receptors are activated, they send high-temperature signals to the brain that the brain is mistaken for the environment or that the body is warming.

In response to this "false heat", the brain quickly activates the heat-dissemination system, including promoting the expansion of the body's blood vessels and causing heavy sweating. In the case of wet-cooled climates in the temperate zone or in the subheat zone, this may be a temporary boost to local blood circulation and a short comfort.

Deadly crisis in a very cold environment: blood vessels spreading and heat loss

However, the situation is completely different when the scene moves to the real cold areas, such as the high-altitude mountains or near the polar circle. In an extremely low-temperature environment, the core survival strategy of the human body is to lock limited heat inside the body and protect core organs such as the heart, the brain and so forth.

At this point, the human body automatically shrinks the surrounding microvasculars and reduces the flow of blood to the skin surface to prevent the heat from spreading. If, at this point in time, a large amount of chili is taken, and papririn expands the body's blood vessels, it will cause a large amount of warm blood to gush to cold surfaces.

This will have two serious consequences: first, the core temperature of the body will decline rapidly because of the rapid flow of heat into the body; and, second, the evaporation of sweat will take a significant amount of heat further. In a very cold environment, this is no less than an increase in the severity of the problem, which greatly increases the risks of temperature loss and freezing.

The warmth that is really needed in the high cold.

For this reason, the relevant nutrition guidelines issued by authorities such as China’s Center for Disease Control and Disease Control clearly state that people working in high-seas environments should refrain from taking large amounts of spicy and irritating food in order not to affect the stability of blood circulation and temperature in their bodies.

The food that really suits the cold is really high heat, protein and fat. Eskimo or northern pastoralists, for example, traditionally take meat and grease, which can provide long-lasting and stable heat through production.

Summarizing: food intelligence, adapted to local conditions

The hot peppers are indeed a wonderful food for the wet and cold in the south, but they are definitely not suitable for the extreme cold. Understanding the biological responses and dietary taboos of different climates will enable us to truly be scientificly nourished and healthy for the winter while enjoying good food.
