Stunning! Our nearest giant neighbor, the Constellation galaxy, hides hundreds of millions of stars and the secrets of future collisions!

Author: Yuki Publication: 10 August 2026 Update: 10 August 2026 Reading time: About 7 minutes

This is definitely one of the most beautiful images in the night sky! On the clear autumn night, as long as you leave the city's lanterns, you can hide in your naked eye a dim dots of cylindrical beams, a fairy constellation about 2.5 million light years away. It is not only the most recent large spiral galaxy outside the galaxy, but also the most remote deep space that humans can see. This huge body, known as M31 or NGC 224, hangs in the direction of the Fairy, conceals about one trillion stars, and secrets linked to the future fate of our galaxy.

The Sensus galaxy is among the largest members of our galaxy, estimated to be 150,000-220,000 light-years in diameter, far beyond the galaxy. Early estimates estimate it to be about twice as big as the Milky Way, but recent studies have shown that the two may be very close to each other, about 1 trillion sun. It is approaching the Milky Way at a rate of about 300 kilometres per second, which has led astronomers to predict for a long time that the two major galaxies will experience spectacular collisions and mergers in the coming billions of years.

Historical findings and confirmations

The observational history of the Sensus galaxy dates back to 964 A.D., when Persian astronomer Abdul-Rahman Soofy recorded this "minus cloud" for the first time in Stars. It was still seen as a nebula in the galaxy. Until 1923 and 1924, astronomer Edwin Hubble, using a 100-inch lens in Mount Wilson, identified the father-forming star in the Sensus galaxy, calculated its distance from beyond the Milky Way system, and officially identified it as an independent extra-river galaxy. This discovery completely changed human understanding of the scale of the universe, proving that the galaxy is not the universe.

Since then, the fairy constellation system has become the best laboratory for astronomers to study nearby galaxies. Its star range is about 3.4, and even amateurs can easily capture their core bright areas with the naked eye or small telescope in the dark sky. The full agular diameter is about six to seven times the full moon, showing an agglomeration of about 77 degrees, almost the angle of a side view.

The mystery of structure and evolution

The Sensus galaxy is classified as the SA(s)b spiral galaxy, and recent observations show that it is actually a great spiral galaxy. It's full of ejections and young blue stars, forming a tight spiral arm. The core region has super-mass black holes, with a mass of about 100 to 140 million suns, and far beyond the center of the galaxy. In addition, it has a host of multiple-guarded galaxies, including the famous M32 and M110 systems, and dozens of darker dwarf galaxies, which form a complex satellite system.

Not only did Hubble's latest 2.5 billion pixel panorama puzzle capture the brightness of 200 million stars, but it also revealed a history of evolution that is very different from that of the galaxy. The evidence shows that during the last billions of years, fairies have experienced many collisions and devourings with the stars, leaving clear stars and young stars. A major merger about 2 to 4 billion years ago may have shaped its current structure, making its stars more dynamic and confusing than the Milky Way.

And the fate of the galaxy.

For a long time, astronomers predicted that the Sensode and Milky Way systems would be colliding around 4 to 5 billion years later, and would eventually merge into a giant cylindrical galaxy called the Galactic Fairy. The most recent study, based on LAMOST and Gaia satellite data, has remeasured the speed of movement, showing a high probability of convergence of about 90 per cent over the next 10 billion years, with a median time of about 6.5 billion years. However, studies have also indicated that the probability is likely to be only about 50 per cent, depending on the gravitational influence of the members of other galaxies. Regardless of the outcome, this cosmic collision will not directly destroy the solar system, but may push the solar system to the outer perimeter of the new galaxy.

In recent years, observations have further revealed the rare event of large-mass stars "silently disappearing" from the formation of a black hole in the Sensula galaxy, as well as the rapid decline of two stars on X-rays, which provide valuable clues to understanding the evolution of stars and the formation of black holes. At the same time, the newly discovered ultra-minor dwarf galaxy, such as Andromeda XXXVI, is 12.5 billion years old and has gained a better understanding of its early history.

The Constellation galaxy is not only a beauty in the night sky, but also a key window that opens up the evolution of galaxies, the distribution of dark matter and the large-scale structure of the universe. Through the continuous observations of Hubble, Spicer, Gaia, and ground-based large telescopes, we are gradually coming up with a full story of this giant neighbor. The next time you look at the autumn sky, look for that light, imagine there's a trillion stars flashing, and a long-distance meeting with our galaxy.

As the technology of observation continues to improve, the fairy galaxy will continue to bring surprises. Whether it is its hidden history of collisions, its system of star protection, or its interaction with the future of the Milky Way, it reminds us that the universe is far more dynamic and beautiful than we thought.
