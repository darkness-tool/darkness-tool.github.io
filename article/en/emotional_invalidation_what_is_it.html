What's "no"? Psychologist to expose the harm you're hiding!

Author: Ling Issued: 1 January 2026 Update: 1 January 2026 Reading time: about 5 minutes

Have you ever been deeply hurt by a "no big deal"? In our daily lives, we often hear words like "Don't cry, why cry?" "You're too sensitive," "It's no big deal." These seemingly consoling words are actually “negative denials” that make each other's feelings invisible, low and even unreasonable. It's not just going to happen to others, but it can come from ourselves, turning a blind eye to our feelings or being harshly critical. This phenomenon, which is psychologically called "Emotional Invalidity" (Emotional Invalidity), can cause deep internal harm and affect human relations and mental health. Many of us, without realizing that they are victims or perpetrators, are here today to reveal the true nature of "rejection" in order to make you see its harm and learn how to avoid it.

A negative mood refers to what happened when one's thoughts, feelings or feelings were rejected (or ignored, criticized or judged by others) to be inappropriate. It makes people feel that their emotions are not valued or allowed to exist. Psychologists pointed out that such denial would create a strong sense of frustration among victims and even doubt that their feelings were normal. Particularly for emotionally sensitive people, denial can cause greater harm, leading to lower self-esteem, anxiety and even depression. The most common examples include parents who say "boys can't cry" to their children, or couples who say "you're in big trouble again." These words, although they are of good will, inadvertently negate each other’s true feelings.

It's the most common form of expression in the negative.

The emotional denial of many hidden forms is often the most painful of these words: the first, "Stop crying, it's nothing" — directly denying each other's grief and making people feel that their tears are not worth it. The second, "You're too sensitive" -- it's not reasonable to suggest that the other side is overreacting. The third, "I wouldn't have done this" — adding to each other with my own criteria, ignoring a difference. The fourth, "Break your head up" — forcing each other to suppress their feelings and deny the pain of the moment. The fifth, "What's this? I've seen worse before" — lower each other's feelings in comparison. These words sound like consolation, but they do make people feel helpless. More seriously, when we get used to self-denial, we internalize these voices and keep telling ourselves, "I shouldn't think so," "I feel wrong," which leads to chronic emotional depression and internal consumption.

Why do you deny it? Because human instincts need to be understood and accepted. When emotions are rejected, we feel we are not valued, and even we begin to doubt our value. Research has shown that long periods of exposure to emotional denial, especially in childhood, can easily lead to low self-esteem, marginality or emotional disorders. Many people turn to depression, appear calm, and have a lot of negative energy in their hearts, which may eventually explode or turn into physical symptoms, such as headaches, insomnia, etc. At the same time, those who impose denials are often themselves denied, creating a vicious cycle.

The consequences of negative emotions and community reflection

The consequences of a denial are far from imaginary, and it not only destroys human relations, but also causes victims to become more and more distraught and afraid to express their true feelings. Many netizens shared their experiences: being disavowed by a partner in intimate relationships eventually led to a break-up; and being "you're too glassy" in the workplace, causing chronic anxiety. Psychologists have called for awareness to be raised to avoid harm. At the same time, learning to give an "emotional test" like, "I understand that you're sad right now, it's normal" and it can improve relations. More and more people in the community are discussing this issue, and there are those who have shared it, encouraging people to look at the emotional harm of childhood; and those who have suggested that schools should be integrated into emotional education, so that the next generation can avoid this invisibility. This reflection not only enhances personal mental health, but also increases the warmth and connection of society as a whole.

What do we do when we lose? In the first place, you can read your own language and avoid speaking to others. Second, when rejected, bravely said, "I want my feelings heard." Third, practice self-censorship: Tell yourself, "My mood is reasonable, it has meaning." Many came out of the shadows through psychological consultations to regain emotional freedom. Specialists say that it is healthy to accept all emotions, be they positive or negative. With more and more discussion, there is a belief that you will be better able to value each other's feelings and give life full of understanding and support.
