Newly recruited workers leave on their first day of work.

Author: Treasure Date issued: 10 September 2026 Update: 10 September 2026 Reading time: about 5 minutes

In anticipation of going to work in a new company, it turns out that the environment or work is completely different from what it looks like, and even in a short day, it's decided to leave immediately. Many of the first-time workers who enter the job market or are in a state of shock have this question: If it's only one day away from work, does the employer have the right to pay nothing?

In fact, this type of situation is not uncommon in the workplace. On the first day of work, some employees found that the company was operating at a different level from the interview, or that they could not continue because of their physical and sudden onset factors, so they chose to leave the job the next day or even the day they closed. On the Internet, the myth of "one day without food" is often heard, but from Hong Kong's statutory perspective, this is a completely erroneous statement.

There's a statutory pay guarantee for one day of work.

According to Hong Kong's Employment Ordinance (Cap. 57), as long as the employee has worked for the employer, even if only a few hours or only one day of work, a real employment relationship has been established between the two parties. As a result, employers must pay their employees their salaries in accordance with the number of days they actually worked, and they must not be deprived of the wages they deserve for their labour on the grounds of “a month of underwork” or “a trial period”.

If the employer refuses to pay for the day ' s labour, it is already legally constituted to be in arrears. Workers have the right to seek assistance from the labour force through legal channels and even from employers to recover the relevant legal interest. Therefore, there is absolutely no need for working children to worry about "doing it for a day."

The first month of the trial, the notice period.

In addition to salaries, the most important concern is often the issue of the notice period. A contract of employment with a regular company would provide for an experimental term. According to the Employment Ordinance, in the first month of the trial period, either party (whether the employer or the employee) may submit the contract “immediately” if they wish to terminate it, without any advance notice period or payment of the notice.

This means that if you feel inappropriate on the first day of work and propose to leave within the first month of the trial period, you can generally do so immediately, without being bound by an ordinary one-month notice period in a contract. But if the contract has no trial period at all, it will be different. I don't know. If the contract does not have a trial period and there is no express notice period, in the absence of a clear provision, both parties may need to be treated in accordance with a statutory or contractual requirement of tacit recognition, and it is therefore important to look closely at the terms of the contract before entering into service.

How many days before the employer can deliver food?

The other key question is when to get the day's labor, when the employees will leave and finish the transfer. According to the Employment Ordinance, the employer must pay the employee in full "for seven days" after the termination of the contract of employment.

In fact, some companies have violated the law by using the excuse that "the accounting process takes one month" or "the next month's food day will be over." If the payment is not received beyond the seven-day deadline, the employee may retain evidence such as attendance records, correspondence conversations and contracts and file a complaint with the Labour Department to safeguard his rights and interests.

In conclusion, the right to leave one day on return to work is absolutely a right to the day's salary. In the face of discomfort in the workplace, working children must be aware of their legal rights and take back their own blood and sweat money through reasonable and legitimate communication to avoid unjustified losses.
