It's amazing how the breathing rate of animals is closely related to life.

Author: Keung Date of issue: 1 May 2026 Update: 1 May 2026 Reading time: About 3 minutes

You know how slow the animals breathe can determine how long they live! In the vast animal kingdoms, from small-breathing mice to large, slow-respiring elephants and turtles, the respiratory frequency of different species shows huge differences. Many scientific observations and studies have found that there is an interesting negative correlation between respiratory frequency and life: the faster the breathing animals, the shorter the life, and the slower the breathing animals, the longer they live. Behind this is a multi-biomechanism of base metabolism, energy consumption and heart rate, which has to be impressed by nature's tact.

Respiration rates are the number of breathings per minute in animals. For mammals, small animals, such as rats, can breathe more than 30 to 60 times a minute, or even more, while large animals, such as elephants, can breathe about 3 to 5 times a minute. The reptiles, like turtles, have a lower rate of breathing, probably two to four times a minute. These differences are not random, but are closely related to the size of the species, metabolic needs and survival strategies. Long-term research by scientists has shown that this breathing rhythm seems to be linked to the total energy consumption of animals throughout their lives.

Specific examples of respiratory and life expectancy

Let's see some specific data. Rats, typically small mammals, have a high respiratory frequency of 30 to 60 per minute, with an average life expectancy of one to three years. By contrast, dogs have about 15 to 35 breathing frequencies per minute, with life expectancy ranging from 10 to 20 years. The cow's breathing frequency is about 20 times a minute, and his life reaches 20 to 30 years. Humans usually have silent breathing frequencies of between 12 and 18 times a minute, with an average life expectancy of about 70 to 80 years. And elephants have only about four to five breathing frequencies per minute, with life expectancy of 60 to 80 years.

More impressive is the reptile. Big turtles breathe about two or four times a minute, with a life expectancy of 150 years or more. Chickens breathe about 30 times a minute and live about 12 years; ducks die about 28 times a minute and 16 years. The monkey's breathing frequency is about 32 times a minute, with an average life expectancy of eight years. These data show that the lower the respiratory frequency, the longer the animal lives. This rule is repeatedly observed in many species.

Scientific interpretation: metabolism and "fixed breathing" hypothesis

Why is the breathing rate related to life? The main reason is the base metabolic rate. Smaller animals have larger relative surfaces, with faster heat spreads, and higher metabolism is required to maintain body temperature, which leads to higher rates of breathing and heartbeat to provide more oxygen and energy. On the contrary, macrofauna units have lower metabolism rates, slower breathing and heart rate and more energy consumption.

Research indicates that the total number of heart beats in many mammals over their lifetime is roughly within a numerical range, for example, about 700 to 2.5 billion. There is a similar pattern of the number of breathings, and the total number of breathings in a lifetime may be relative. This means that animals with a high respiratory frequency will be able to "exhaust" more quickly the number of breaths available in their lifetime, and life will be shorter. In the scientific paper, it is mentioned that the rate of respiratory and heart rate is directly related, and the rate of heart rate is related to the failure of life, a chain that underpins the "Rate of Living Theory".

In addition, slower breathing helps to reduce oxidizing stress and inflammation reactions and to protect cells and tissues from damage. Rapid breathing may increase free-base generation and accelerate ageing. The reptiles and the two herds of animals suffer from lower metabolism and very slow breathing, so that many species, such as some turtles or crocodiles, can live a hundred years, and even display the phenomenon of “neglecting ageing”.

A comparison of different animal species.

Among mammals, this is the most obvious. Small teeth, such as hamsters, have a high respiratory frequency of about 80 per minute and a life of only about 2 years; large mammals, such as whales, are very slow to breathe and can live longer than 200 years. Although birds have a high metabolic rate, some species are more efficient because of their special respiratory system (aerobic cystal system) and there are life differences. The reptiles generally breathe slowly and live longer, which is associated with their warming properties, with lower energy consumption.

It's interesting to note that humans are somewhat special in this rule. Although the rate of human breathing is not very slow, we live far more than many closely sized mammals through improved medicine, technology and lifestyles. This means that intelligent creatures can break the laws of nature through outside intervention.

In any case, there is a clear correlation between the breathing length of animals and their life expectancy, which helps us not only to understand the evolutionary and physiological mechanisms of life, but also to inspire human health. For example, learning slow breathing techniques may help to reduce stress and improve CPR functions. Of course, life expectancy is influenced by a variety of factors, such as genetics, environment, food, and so on, and respiratory frequency is only one of the important indicators. In the future, scientific research will continue to explore this mystery in depth, providing additional clues for the extension of life. The animal phenomenon reminds us that adaptation slows down, perhaps the natural path to longevity.
