What do you mean, "over"? So it's not just about the dead!

By May. Date of issue: 11 August 2026 Update: 11 August 2026 Reading time: About 9 minutes

One of the most common words of Chinese mourning is "excessive." Whenever a relative dies, family members often ask a monk or a monk to read the book, with the aim of exceeding the dead. So, what do you mean, "over"? Is it only about the dead? Or is there a deeper religious and cultural dimension? This paper will provide you with a full and detailed analysis of the true meaning of "excess" from the sources of the words, the explanations of Buddhism and Taoism, the content of the rituals, and the modern meaning of the term.

According to the dictionary and the classics, the word "over" is "chāo" and the note is "sweet"? This term was first found in the Tri-Leo, which was intended to mean "jumping" or "crossing" a barrier, such as jumping over a bridge. In Tang Dynasty, it began to refer to "over" or "over" as, for example, "over-the-senior" as being beyond peers or previous generations. In the period of Song Won, as Buddhism and Taoism flourished among the people, they became more and more religiously speaking, and helped the souls of those who died out of their misery through readings, practices, etc.

In Buddhism and Taoism.

In Buddhism and Taoism, excesses (also written as overture, super-referral, recommendation, recommendation, extraction) are the laws of prayer for the dead. The Buddhist scripture, like the Book of the Hide, states that when a man dies, if he has created a more evil business and no one is recommended to him, he may be born to the three evil ways of hell, the hungry and the animals. Especially in the seven seventy-seven hundred and forty-nine days after his death, if his family returns to the dead with good will, it will help him to avoid falling into evil, and to return to the world of good. That's what they call "excess." It began with Liang Wu-Di's Zhulan Tsang, which is a lyrical ulambana in the Sanskrit language, which means "saving down" , which is to save the lives of the dead and hungry.

In the case of Taoistism, it is equally important to save the dead. The concept of "deep in hell" already exists in the Taoist scriptures, such as the Five Books of the Death of the Spirit. Through their in-house training and connections to the ancestor or the fairy, the Taoist has pulled out the dead from the shadows or the rest of the world, hoping that they will soon enter the world of great joy in the east. Taoism is often given to Taib's God of Saviour, and ritual forms can be divided into 10 different types (orphans in the vicinity), the ancients (for their ancestors) and the wrong spirits (for the dead, the infants or the wrong relatives).

The actual content and timing of the excess ritual

In the Chinese funeral, excesses are often referred to as "congratulations" or "contributions". Their families will send monks or monks to read the Book or the Book, and they will call upon the Buddha to make amends for those who have died, to increase the reward, and to ease the suffering in hell or in the hungry. The most common Buddhist scriptures include the Books of the Land, the Amitah, the Book of the King, the Book of the Heart, the Great Curse, etc. There are also large rituals of worship, such as the Book of the Beam, the Water Curse, or the burning of mouths to feed, and the Water and Land Congress. The Taoists often recited the Book of Mankind and the Book of the Nativity. There are sometimes symbolic rituals of "break the hell" that follow the story of a woman who saves her eyes and saves the dead from hell.

The most important time is seven seventy-nine days after death. According to the public opinion, the mid-massive body (the state of life between death and birth) was scheduled for seven days, seven times before the official verdict of the forty-ninth day of the crime. So every seven days from the "first seven" to the "full seven" do the law. The South is particularly concerned with the tradition of "seven." Even after forty-nine days, future generations may continue to hold super-referendations for their ancestors on the Day of Chong Ming, Chinese Won or the Day of Monument.

It's not just for the dead, it's for his own good.

Many people think that the excesses are merely a dead spirit, but Buddhists actually have a broader interpretation of the "oversteps." The real excesses have three meanings: first, the excesses of the mind, the return of the conceited or the illusory, to the truth and to the consciousness; secondly, the excesses of the present life, which, through the teachings of life and death, free from the six wheels of death; and thirdly, the excesses of the good and the afterlife, where the Book is recited to the dead, which will help to bring them to life. In other words, the ultimate goal of the excesses is "to go beyond life and death and to join the other side."

It is clear from the Book that those who commit excesses to the dead, the dead in seven shall receive only one share, and the living shall receive six. This means that not only those who have lost their interests, but also those who are alive, have great merit. It reflects the spirit of "self-interest" in Buddha's Law: helping the dead to escape their misery, on the one hand, and purifying their handicaps and increasing their goodness through compassion and piety on the other. Mr. Sun Zhongshan has also invoked the term "excessive" as a moral aid, which is a metaphor for the assistance of a troubled group, a term that has reached the spiritual level of Chinese culture.

It is worth noting that the success of excesses is not by the ritual itself. Buddhists are tough to regulate, and the Anglicans can't beat it. Those who have exceeded them need to be sincere and moral, and those who have died need to have a heart of repentance and good, so that they will be able to achieve a higher degree of success. So it's a magic of "securing the dead to go to heaven" rather than a way of helping the dead and the living to grow up together, through virtue and compassion.

Conclusion: Understanding excess is understanding the Chinese attitude to life and death.

It goes from "jumping through barriers" to religious rituals, which help the soul to transcend suffering, to spiritual transcendence and relief. It reflects the deep attachment of Chinese culture to life and death: death is not the end; it is the beginning of another journey; the living express their goodness and compassion through rituals, and at the same time cleanse their hearts. Whether you believe in religion or not, understanding the true meaning of "extraordinary" will make us more gentle in the face of life's uncertainty and in the broader sense of the link between life and death.
