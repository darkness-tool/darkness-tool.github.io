It's a moral dilemma. What's the question of a train? Pull the pole to save five or watch five die?

Author: Haru Date of issue: 7 May 2026 Update: 7 May 2026 Reading time: About 7 minutes

One out of control train is about to kill five workers, and if you pull down the lever, you can turn it to another track and sacrifice only one person ... What would you do? This is the world-renowned "Trolley Problem" - an intellectual experiment that seems simple but profoundly test human morality. Since 1967, when the British philosopher Philippa Ford introduced it, it has become the most popular subject of discussion in the realm of philosophy, psychology, ethics and even artificial intelligence. Is utilitarianism's “best happiness” right, or is it more important to insist on the moral principle of “no one can kill by himself”? Let us step by step explore this moral dilemma of never-behind-standard answers.

Imagine an ordinary afternoon when you're standing near the control station by the track. All of a sudden, a brake-bus broke out at high speed and five workers on the front track were concentrating on work and were completely unaware of the imminent danger. They'll be killed in seconds by a car. At this point, you notice that there's a control pole next to it, and if you pull it off, the train turns to another turnoff, and there's only one worker working on that track. If you do nothing, five will die; if you pull down the lever, only one will die. Would you choose to pull the pole? That's the classic version of the tram problem.

The Origin and Origin of the Trail

The question of the motorbike was first addressed by British philosopher Philippa Foot. In 1967, it was presented in the paper Abortion and the Double Effects Act. Ford originally designed this situation to explore such issues as abortion and the "Doctrine of Double Effects". Later, American philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson and others developed a variety of variations to make the issue widely known. The idea experiment has not only generated intense discussion in the academic world, but has become a classic tool to test moral instincts in public culture, movies, Ailen.

Extremist vs.

Most people, in the face of the classic tram problem, choose to pull down the lever and sacrifice one to save five. This reflects the idea of Utilatarianism, spearheaded by philosophers Blanche and John Mill, that the fight for action depends on whether the outcome will lead to the greatest overall happiness. It seems a rational choice to sacrifice one person for five to survive and to save four lives. The opposition, however, proceeded from the point of view of the Deontology, arguing that "no one can be used as an instrument for an end" and that the pull-in was tantamount to a voluntary killing, which was morally forbidden. Even if the result is to save more people, it should not violate the fundamental principle of "not killing innocents".

Psychological studies show that about 90 per cent of the respondents opt for a pull in the classic version. But when the situation changes, people's choices are very high. This shows that human morality is not purely rational, but deeply influenced by emotion, intuition and circumstances.

Famous variant: Push the fat guy off the bridge.

Tomson's "fat" variant is even more challenging: you stand on the bridge and see a runaway train crashing five workers. There's a very fat man standing next to him, and if you push him off the bridge, his body can block the train and save five people, but the fat guy dies. Will you push him? In this version, most people refuse to push because this is a direct, hand-in-hand threat to kill an innocent bystander, not just a "transfer" threat. The difference highlights the moral difference between "killing" and "killing" and the "double-effect principle": the perceived side effects are different from the intentional harm.

More Variables and Practical Applications

There are also many variations on the subject, such as the version of Fat Bad: Would you be more willing to pull if the five on the track were innocent and if the turn-off was bad people who killed your family? In the past few years, there has been a great deal of discussion about the situation, and it has been a great deal of discussion. These changes help philosophers explore personal relationships, intentions, responsibilities, etc. In the modern world, the issue of motor vehicles is widely applied to the design of auto-piloting: who should AI protect first when accidents are inevitable? Passengers or pedestrians? In addition, in areas such as medical ethics, war decisions, organ transplants, etc., similar dilemmas can be seen.

The question of the motor vehicle is not exactly the right answer, but it's the essence of what leads us to reflect on our own moral framework. Some believe that in extreme circumstances the outcome is paramount, and some insist that there is no compromise. Regardless of the choice, this ideological experiment reminds us that moral decisions are often filled with grey areas and require consideration of the consequences, intentions, rights and responsibilities. Philosophers are still arguing, and everyone who thinks about it may have a deeper understanding of humanity.

Next time you hear something like a moral dilemma, ask yourself: Will your hand reach the lever at the moment the train comes? This seemingly abstract question is actually testing the difficult choices that each of us may face in the real world. Let's continue this endless moral debate!
