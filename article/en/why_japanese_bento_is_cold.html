Unbelievable! Why is Japanese lunch always cold? Unmasked cultural secrets!

Author: Da Ming Date of issue: 5 September 2026 Update: 5 September 2026 Reading time: About 4 minutes

This is a very curious situation for many foreign tourists arriving in Japan. Whether it's a convenience store, a train that's being sold at the station, or a Japanese mother's own, it's almost cold. Why do Japanese people get used to cold lunch? In fact, it is deeply rooted in culture and wisdom.

For many people who are used to eating hot, hot, hot, hot food, it seems hard to imagine cold food. In Japan, however, cold lunches are not just a habit of eating and eating, but also an extraordinary aesthetic and social ritual that has evolved over centuries.

The historical source of cold lunch in Japan.

Japan's cold-food culture can be traced back to the ancient times of Naryan and peace. In response to the need for long travels or war, people began to dry up their rice or make it easy for them to carry it, so that people could add to their power at any time, and this habit was gradually transforming into a later culture.

Why not heat up? Food and conservation science.

Japan’s direct cold food is about its unique cooking and choice of food. Most of the Japanese lunches reduce moisture, and often use vinegar, sugar, salt, etc., for larvae, or make explosives, which are difficult to breed even at normal temperatures.

In addition, many of the associated vegetables, such as salad, prune or fruit, are not in themselves suitable for heating. If you take the whole lunch to the microwave to heat it, it not only destroys the taste of the food, but it may also mix up the smell of the vegetables, destroying the taste of the food.

To be kind to others.

In addition to the scientific considerations of the food itself, "no one is disturbed" is at the heart of Japan's cold lunch culture. In public places such as Japanese offices, trains or schools, the fragrance of food may disturb the surroundings if it is hotened.

Eating cold lunches avoids the sound of microwaves and the smell of food, displaying Japanese etiquette and high self-discipline.

It tastes like food.

It also changes the way people taste food. Hot food stimulates appetite with a strong scent, while cold rice and cooking makes it easier to chew the sweetness of the grain itself and the natural taste of the food. The next time you visit Japan, you might want to buy a delicious railroad and experience this unique food culture.
