Surprise! Why can't some people get out of a bad mood? Nobody wants to be sad!

No one likes to be sad, but why can't someone get out of a bad mood? In our daily lives, we sometimes encounter low-tide moments, but some seem to be unable to get rid of negative emotions. Whether they are depressed, anxious or blameful, they are trapped in an invisible cage, chewing on the pain of the past and unable to move forward. This is not only personal suffering, but also the incompetence of relatives and friends around them. Psychological studies have shown that this is not simply a "deep mind" and that deep biological, psychological and environmental factors are behind it. This post will be taken from an evolutionary and psychological perspective, reveal the secret of negative bias and explore ways to think back about how to become a vicious cycle. At the same time, we will discuss the causes of genetics, childhood experiences, etc., and provide practical advice to help move out of the mood. In fact, negative emotions are not bad in themselves; they are the product of human evolution, to warn us of our potential dangers. But when they zoom in too much, they become invisible killers of mental health. Many thought that "time will cure everything," but if we don't understand the root causes, the negative mood will only grow. Let's explore why some people are particularly vulnerable to negative emotions and how to effectively break the cycle. It helps us not only by helping ourselves, but also by making us more aware of those who look like glass hearts. Turns out, the "negative mirror" in the head is much stronger than I thought. But the good news is, we have a chance to retrain the brain when we know why. It's not an inexplicable sort of scavenger. Are you ready to tell the truth together?

In the first place, the human brain is inherently “negativity bias” in terms of evolutionary psychology. This is the key mechanism for ancestors to survive in dangerous environments: negative events (e.g. in the case of beasts) are more life-threatening than positive events (e.g. finding food), so the brain takes priority, remembers and magnifies negative information. While modern society is much safer, the system still works, making it easier for us to remember criticism rather than praise, magnify failure and ignore success. Research shows that negative irritation is much more powerful than positive irritation, which explains why a negative word is counterproductive to many positive words. This bias has made some people more sensitive to negative sentiments and prone to continued decline. In addition, negative bias can affect decisions, lead to excessive risk avoidance, missed opportunities, and further reinforce incompetence. While evolution gives us an advantage for survival, it also creates emotional burdens. Fortunately, with that understanding, we can deliberately balance the angles. Negative bias, though deep-rooted, must not be changed. It's a common human mind bug, not a weakness.

Retrospect: the vicious cycle of negative emotions

The other major killer is "reflecting" , which means chewing back and forth negative events, causes and consequences, rather than a positive solution. According to the psychologist Nolen-Hoeksema, anti-repression magnifies depression and anxiety and creates a vicious cycle: the harder it gets, the harder it gets. "Why am I so unhappy? "What did I do wrong?", which not only helps solve the problem, but also activates more negative memories. The study found that the backlash is the result of a hyperactivity of the brain-based default mode network (DMN), which is stuck in a self-centred negative narrative. This is particularly common among persons suffering from depression, leading to longer-term symptoms. It also interferes with attention and implementation, making day-to-day matters more difficult to handle and deepening the sense of helplessness. Interestingly, aberrations are mistaken for "deep thinking," but it's actually passive. The socialization of women makes them more vulnerable to anti-retroactive patterns. Long-term reverses and even changes in the structure of the brain, strengthening negative paths. But the good news is that through a method of recognition of behaviour, this cycle can be effectively interrupted. Understanding the nature of the reaction is the first step out of the negative mood. Many people are obscuring and realizing that this is a great improvement.

Legacy, childhood and environmental impacts

In addition to evolutionary and cognitive factors, genetic and early experiences play a key role. Research has shown that depression has a genetic orientation, and the abnormal serotonin system makes it easier to be sensitive to negative irritation. Childhood adversity (e.g. abuse, neglect) shapes the voice of internal criticism, leaving adults vulnerable to blame and negative feelings. The growing environment of excessive protection can also lead to a lack of capacity to deal with setbacks and collapse in the event of a disaster. Today's high-pressure, social media is more cultural, further magnifying negative biases and creating the feeling that “others are better off”. The epidemic and other major events have made many people more emotional. The combination of these factors makes it particularly difficult for some to get out of the low tide. But it's not fate. The day after tomorrow's intervention can change brain plasticity. Professional help, such as psychotherapy, can re-establish health mood patterns. Environmental support is also important, and family and friends understand less solitude. In the end, getting out of the negative mood needed self-help and his help.

How to break the shackles of negative emotions?

Although the reasons are complex, it is not impossible to get out of the negative. First, practice "receptive": admit negative emotions, not repress them or judge them bad. Research has shown that acceptance reduces the intensity of the mood and naturally reduces it. Second, the break-up: when you realize you're thinking about things, you turn your attention away, like sports, meditation or talking to friends. Learning is particularly effective to train the brain in the present, not the past or the future. Question yourself, "Is this true?" "Do you have any evidence?" Sports and good sleep are also powerful helpers for emotional adjustment. If the symptoms are serious, seeking professional help like the CBT, the effect is clear. Through these methods, many have succeeded in coming out of the low tide and re-emerging. Remember, negative moods like clouds will eventually pass. Stick to practice, the brain tends to be positive.
