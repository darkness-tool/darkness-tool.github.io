Every night, there are fireworks in the sky, which always attracts many wonders and eyes. However, this short-lived and ferocious photo-show does not appear in the air, but is a combination of precision chemical reactions and physical mechanics. How does a small pyrotechnic bomb turn into a pattern and colour in the air?

To understand the mystery of fireworks, first of all, it needs to be broken down. A standard high-altitude pyrotechnic is usually made of shell, fuse, firing powder and internal fireworks. The outer shell protects the inside chemical agent from the outside environment, while the fuse is responsible for lighting the entire reaction at the right time.

Lifting power: the power of the oxidation reaction

When the fuses on the ground are lit, the black powder at the bottom is quickly ignited. Black gunpowder, as an energy source for fireworks, is composed mainly of nitric acid, sulphur and charcoal. When these components come into contact, there will be an acute oxidation response, which will release a large amount of heat and high-pressure gas in an instant.

This powerful push will shoot fireworks like rockets at hundreds of metres high. At this point, the time-defining time-deficit fuses are burning, and when the fireworks reach their pre-determined peak, the fuses will ignite the internal explosive.

The Secret of the Color: The Flaming Reaction of Metals

When the internal explosives explode, the stars embedded in them, that is, small particles containing metal compounds, are scattered around. These metal ions absorb energy at high temperatures, their electrons rise to higher energy levels, and when the electrons return to their matrices they release specific wavelength energy in the form of light, which is the famous flame reaction.

For example, thorium is fresh red when it burns, thorium is green, and thorium is golden. As for the most difficult blues, it takes precision control of temperature and a combination of copper compounds to be perfect.

In addition to colour, the burning of metal or beryllium powder produces bright white light and silver white light, while iron powder produces orange and yellow sparks like meteors. This rich mix of materials gives the possibility of an endless vision of fireworks.

As mentioned above, fireworks are not just a decoration for festivals, but also a product of the perfect integration of science and art. The next time you look at the night sky again and enjoy this visual feast, you can look at the fascinating physics and chemistry behind your taste.
