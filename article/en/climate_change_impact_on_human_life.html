Shake! Climate change has completely changed human life! Your next meal and your next breath could be in danger?

By Jade. Date of issue: 7 May 2026 Update: 7 May 2026 Reading time: About 4 minutes

Climate change is changing our way of life! In this seemingly ordinary time, a global crisis caused by human activity is accelerating. Climate change not only leads to rising global temperatures, but also to the associated effects of extreme weather events, rising sea levels and declining agricultural production, which directly and profoundly affect human health, the economy, the living environment and daily life. According to World Health Organization and United Nations reports, climate change has become one of the greatest health threats of the twenty-first century, with the potential to kill hundreds of thousands of people each year and to drive hundreds of millions of people into poverty and hunger. How can this change affect our future step by step?

The main drivers of climate change are the large emissions of greenhouse gases, including carbon dioxide and methane, from fossil fuel combustion, deforestation and industrial activities. The global average temperature has risen by about 1.1°C over the past century, but it is only the beginning. Scientists predict that, without immediate reduction, the temperature could rise by more than 2°C or higher by the end of the century. This will lead to an irreversible chain reaction, from the melting of polar glaciers to the extreme drying of the hot zone, and will not fail to reshape human social structures.

Extremely high temperatures and direct human health killing. Hands.

With global warming, heat waves become more frequent, intense and persistent. Between 1998 and 2017, more than 166,000 people worldwide died of heat-related diseases. The summer heat in Europe in 2022 caused more than 61 million deaths. The heat not only causes heat, heat failure, cardiovascular disease, but also increases respiratory problems. The elderly, children and chronically ill are the most vulnerable groups. Studies show that the number of heat-related deaths among people over 65 has increased by 70 per cent over the past 20 years. In addition, high temperatures increase air pollution and ozone intensity and further degenerate lung health.

Not only is climate change changing patterns of disease transmission. The expansion of areas suitable for the survival of vectors such as mosquitoes has led to the spread of infectious diseases such as dengue fever and malaria. According to World Health Organization data, the number of dengue cases rose from 500,000 in 2000 to 5.2 million in 2019. Floods and drought also increase the risk of water-borne diseases such as cholera, especially in areas with poorer sanitation conditions.

Agricultural and food security challenges.

The impact of climate change on agriculture has been severe. A change in precipitation patterns, drought-prone and extreme temperatures has led to a decline in global crop production. The Inter-Government Committee on Climate Change (IPCC) reports that climate change has a negative impact on agricultural crops in most regions, and that while high-level areas may benefit in the short term, food security is at risk overall. Ocean warming and acidification have also hit the fishing industry, with billions of people relying on a rapidly declining ocean resource. By 2050, climate change is expected to push more people into poor nutrition and hunger, especially in developing countries.

Moreover, the food price boom will increase social inequality. Poor people have reduced purchasing power, poor nutrition has worsened, and the consequences of slow child birth and low immunization will continue for generations. Animals are also affected by the heat, with lower yields and a vicious cycle.

Sea-level rise, extreme weather and environmental change

The melting of glaciers has led to sea level rise and coastal cities and low-lying island countries are inundated. Many densely populated areas, such as Bangladesh and Maldives, are facing serious threats. Storm surges, floods and cyclones have increased in intensity, causing damage to homes, infrastructure and human casualties. Over the past decade, about 23.1 million people have been displaced each year as a result of weather-related events, creating "climate refugees".

The lack of water resources is also a major problem. In some areas, reduced precipitation and increased evaporation have resulted in inadequate freshwater supply, affecting water security and irrigation in agriculture. Drought and desertification have further reduced arable land. These changes are not only a threat to survival, but also a source of competition and even social conflict.

Mental health and the profound impact of the economy

The impact of climate change on mental health is often overlooked. High temperatures, disasters and uncertainty increase anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress syndrome. Suicide rates have also increased in some areas due to high temperatures. On the economic front, the decline in agricultural production, damage and spending on health care will drag down global GDP, with the poor being the most affected. The World Bank estimates that tens of millions of people may be in extreme poverty due to climate-related health problems by 2030. In the face of these challenges, individuals and the world need to act together to reduce carbon emissions and improve adaptive capacity in order to protect the future of humanity. Climate change is everywhere, and each of our options will determine the fate of the next generation.
