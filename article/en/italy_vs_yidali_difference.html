"Italian" or "Italian"? The truth is stupid.

By May. Date of issue: 2 August 2026 Update: 2 August 2026 Reading time: About 7 minutes

In the same European country, why do Chinese writings appear in both Italian and Italian terms? This is not a simple misword problem, but a profound difference in the usage of words, historical translation sources and linguistic transliteration rules across the Straits. For the first time, many people notice the problem, and they stop: Why does Taiwan’s official and media use Italy, while China’s mainland and Hong Kong are used to write “Italy”? The difference between the word and the word lies behind more than 400 years of translation history, as well as the choice of a standard for the translation of foreign names from different regions.

First of all, it has to be made clear that whether it is "Italy" or "Italian", it means the same country — the Italian Republic in southern Europe, with boots in shape, the official language is Italian, the capital of Rome. The two writings are identical in their pronunciation, and they read "What's the word?" and "Italia" in Italian. The real difference is only in the choice of the Chinese word: the first word is "mean" or "use". This choice is not random, it is a long-term regional word for word.

Source of history: The first translation to appear is "Italia".

If you trace the original Chinese translation of the country, you can find that the word "mean" actually appears earlier. The Italian preacher Matteo Ricci has clearly marked “Idaria” in his “Text of the Nation” for the 30th anniversary of Ming Dynasty (1602 A.D.). This map was one of the first in the history of China to present a modern-day world, when the Italian Peninsula was not yet unified and was still made up of several cities and states, but Lima has used the term "Italia" to refer to the entire half of the island. At Chiang Dynasty, the translation of Tsui Jijio (in 1849) was extended to the region as "the Italian nation". These early contributions clearly indicate that the original Chinese translation was based on the word "mean" and that it responded to the Italian word "Italia".

The change to "Italian" in Taiwan was not due to a better voice, but rather to the official translation criteria that evolved after the mid-20s. In Taiwan, the Ministry of Education and the press have been using the word "fiction" for a long time, making Italy a standard Chinese language for Taiwan. In contrast, the Chinese mainland regularly uses "Italy" under a simplified word system, while Hong Kong is used to "Italy" because it is influenced by the mainland and the UK for a long time. This reality is reflected in the fact that the Chinese version of Wikipedia is aimed at "Italian" and, in particular, "The Chinese mainland, Hong Kong, Italy".

Why are there two ways to write? The twin effects of speech and politics

When interpreting foreign names, Chinese must select the words close to the original in a limited number of Chinese characters. The first syllable of Italian "Italia" is close to "ee" or "yi" and can be used in Chinese for the words "mean" , "use" , "I" and "I". The early translators chose the word "mean" and then Taiwan changed the word "myth" to "myth," which is identical in the modern language and in Mandarin, so there is no absolute "who is right" standard. Some people think that the word "punishment" has a positive link to "justity" and that the word "means" is more neutral; it has also been pointed out that, if it were to be simplified, the Italian character would be less beautiful and the mainland would therefore maintain the word "Italy". All of these arguments are retrospect, and the real determining factor is still a long-standing usage of words.

It is worth noting that in recent years some young people in Hong Kong have been influenced by Taiwan's online culture and have begun to rewrite Italy in social media. This is a phenomenon that shows that the usage of words is not the same and that it flows with cultural exchange. However, Hong Kong is still dominated by “Italy” in official documents, press reports and official forums. In the same vein, the "Italian" writing in Taiwan is mostly considered to be affected by the mainland or misinputting, and does not meet local standards.

What should we do with it?

For ordinary readers and writers, the most practical principle is "to go to the country." The use of "Italian" is most appropriate when publishing articles in Taiwan, writing travel plans or communicating with readers in Taiwan; in the Chinese mainland or Hong Kong, the term "Italy" is used. If the article needs to be faced with readers on both sides of the Strait, another type of writing can be indicated in brackets at the time of its first appearance, such as "Italian (the Chinese mainland and Hong Kong called Italy)", followed by a single writing to avoid confusion in reading. The academic paper or encyclopedia should follow the official translation criteria of the region.

In addition to the country's name itself, the vocabulary will change. In Taiwan, it says "Italian noodles", "Italian," "Italian", "Italian", "Italian," while in the mainland and Hong Kong it is "Italian noodles", "Italian" and "Italian". This combination has become part of the local language habits, and the imposition of one language is more likely to lead to unnecessary controversy. Understanding differences is more important than arguing which is more right.

Conclusion: the difference between a word and a reflection of the plurality of language

The difference between “Italy” and “Italian” is essentially a Chinese translation custom developed in different regions, not a problem. From Rimar's "Idaria" to today's diversion, this process reflects the movement of the Chinese translation history and reminds us that the language itself is a live system that changes over time and across regions. The next time you see these two writings, smile: the same country with the same boots has two "official" names in Chinese. Whichever one you are used to, the point is always to communicate well, not to insist that your side is the only truth.

Language differences are never an obstacle, but a window to understanding each other's cultures. The next time we see news or travel articles about Italy and Italy, we can judge the author's background in a more comfortable way, and we can appreciate the wealth of Chinese in different regions. This seemingly small word difference is actually the best testimony to the diversity of the Chinese world.
