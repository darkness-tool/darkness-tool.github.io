It's amazing that the Taiwan Rings don't rely on the ocean for power? There's more potential than a few nuclear plants. Why not?

Author: Ah Hau Date of issue: 23 November 2025 Update: 23 November 2025 Reading time: About 9 minutes

It's an amazing energy mystery! Taiwan is surrounded by the sea, a unique source of marine resources, and little mention of "oceanic power" in the energy-transformation wave. According to an ICSR assessment in 2015, the entire ocean has a potential of nearly 10 GW, with currents of 4 GW, temperature differentials of 2.8 GW, wave power of 2.4 GW and tidal power of 0.2 GW. This potential is equivalent to nearly two times the total output of three nuclear power plants in Taiwan (about 5.1 GW) and even exceeds the steady capacity of several nuclear power plants. However, why is Taiwan not actively developing this Sea Gold Mountain? Is it technical? Or environmental and policy barriers? Today, we come to unmask the veil to make you understand the true face of ocean energy. Perhaps this will change your vision of the future of Taiwan green. Let's explore the story behind this blue treasure.

Taiwan is located in a hot zone, with vast waters around it, with strong currents of black currents and temperatures above 20°C in the east, conditions that make the ocean an ideal source of renewable energy. Ocean energy is more stable than solar, wind and weather, and can be used as a power base. The International Energy Agency estimates that global ocean energy can generate 93,100 TWh per year, well above the global total of 17,400 TWh. Taiwan ' s good use is sufficient to significantly reduce its dependence on imported fossil fuels, which currently account for nearly 98 per cent of energy. However, development has been slow, and so far there are only small demonstration projects, such as the 5KW-temperature power generation unit of florist. This is not just a technical challenge, but a test of policy and investment. Imagine that, if the ocean were fully online, Taiwan's carbon emissions would be significantly reduced. Why did you stop? The answer lies in the next analysis. Keep reading. You'll find the truth.

The kind of ocean power and the potential of Taiwan.

Ocean energy is divided into wave, current, temperate and tidal energy, each with a unique advantage. Waves can use waves to drive turbines, with potential fields including the sea off the north-east corner and the lake, estimated at 2.4 GW. The current is based on currents such as black tides, with 4 GW potential in the rich corners and east, the greatest highlight. Temperature differentials (OTEC) use surface and deep sea temperatures to generate electricity, with a potential of 2.8 GW for florist and the sea off Taiwan. Tide can be only 0.2 GW small, but it is suitable for the outer islands of the Golden Gate. It's almost 10 GW, twice the sum of nuclear plants one, two, three. One GW is about 800 million degrees a year, equivalent to a medium nuclear power plant. Half of Taiwan's development could replace the role of nuclear power. But it's only at the research stage, not on a large scale. Why isn't this potential awakening?

According to experts, the black tide on the eastern coast of Taiwan is up to 1-2 knots and is suitable for a floating wheel. Professor Onwinkai of the Maritime University of Taiwan suggested that we start from a technically mature wave and start from shallow to deep. ICAR has established a small OTEC system with 1.2 KW at 14°C temperature difference, which has proved to be feasible. But to reach the economic scale, at least 10 MW is needed, and the challenge is now to be massive and resistant. In contrast to nuclear power, the ocean is free of radiation risk and nuclear waste, but also provides stable power. Why not go this way? Environmental and seismic factors are key. In Taiwan, there are cyclones and earthquakes, and extreme weather weather is required for the development of the sea. These obstacles keep investment stalling.

Nuclear power comparison: why not choose ocean power?

Taiwan now has a total of 5.1 GW of nuclear plants I, II and III, which generate about 40 billion degrees of electricity annually, representing 8-10 per cent of total electricity. If activated, an additional 2.6 GW could be added, but the Fukushima accident and the anti-nuclear wave were sealed. The ocean has 10 GW potential, equal to 5-6 nuclear power plants, with no security concerns. Nuclear power plants take 8-10 years at a cost of hundreds of billions of dollars, and back-end nuclear waste disposal costs $0.1-0.29 per dose. The oceans, though initially highly invested, are free of waste and long-term economic growth. At the policy level, as much as 20 per cent of non-nuclear gardens drive solar, wind and ocean energy. Experts have called for the inclusion of national energy plans, along with offshore wind and electricity. If the ocean's power is ignored, Taiwan's neighbours like Japan are afraid of being green. Japan's Black Tide Project has tested a 500KW team with mirrors in Taiwan. This shows that the ocean is not the next, but the first. It's an emergency!

The development of the oceans can face three major challenges: technical bottle necks, environmental impacts and financial shortfalls. Large cold water pipes are perishable and facilities under the hurricane are at high risk of damage. In a nutshell, cruises can harm marine life and electromagnetic fields affect fish migration. But research has shown that the impact is manageable and far less than the risk of nuclear power. The government has launched a national project to invest in wave and black-tide technology. In the future, if these are overcome, the ocean will become green power. Community and expert enthusiasm: The construction of the demonstration area should be accelerated. If the lake waves can be tested, they can lead to local economies. In the end, it's not just electricity, it's an enduring promise.

Future perspectives and calls

The story of ocean power allows us to reflect on the wisdom of energy choices. Taiwan should not rely solely on imported fuel or nuclear power, but should embrace its own marine treasure. In 2030, the ocean could contribute 5 GW if it was supported by policy. This will reduce carbon emissions, create jobs and enhance energy autonomy. The government has been called upon to invest more, and business cooperation has accelerated. People can also focus on supporting green energy conversions. Little change, bring the sea together. Let's watch the blue future together!
