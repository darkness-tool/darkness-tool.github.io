It's amazing! Santa has a green version? The amazing turn of events from green to red!

By May. Date of issue: 1 May 2026 Update: 1 May 2026 Reading time: About 9 minutes

You think Santa is always a classic red hat? It's not! In the modern sense, Santa always wears a fresh red coat, wears a red hat, has a white beard and sends gifts on a reindeer sleigh on Christmas night. But do you know that in earlier European legends and the Victorian era, Santa Claus or Father Christmas often appeared in green robes? This green version of Santa Claus is not only a symbol of life and spring, but also a reflection of the evolution of the image of Christmas from ancient cults to modern business. Today let us open up this new and well-known Christmas secret and explore why Santa was once green and how he became our familiar red.

Santa's prototype dates back to the fourth-century Turkish Bishop of St. Nicholas. He was a merciful bishop, often secretly helping the poor, especially children, and was therefore honoured as the guardian of children. The original image of St. Nicholas, usually dressed in the Cardinal's red robe, has become assimilated over time with the winter celebrations and heresy traditions across Europe. In England and the European continent, the role of Father Christmas began to develop on its own, and he was no longer a bishop, but a man of festivities and generosity.

The ancient origins and symbolic meaning of the green Santa.

Why does Santa have a green version? It starts with the 17th century European customs. At that time, people liked to decorate their homes with greener plants such as winter, steroids and Ivy during winter and Christmas. These green plants, which remain vibrant in the cold winter, are believed to be magically powerful, to bring good luck, to fight the cold, and to herald spring. As a result, Santa Claus, who is considered to have the power of magic and mercy, is naturally portrayed as wearing a green robe or a green cloak, perhaps with a green leaf on his head.

In the tradition of England, Father Christmas is often painted as a tall man in a green-coloured coat, with a green branch or glass of wine in his hand, representing the joy of the festival and the coming spring. This green image continued into the 19th century, and even in the famous writer Charles Dickens ' classic novel, "A Christmas Carol," the Ghost of Christmas Present was portrayed as a generous giant in a green robe, leading the warmth and sharing spirit of Sculptor Skuki's testimony day.

The many ways in which Santa's image changes.

In fact, before the 19th century, Santa's appearance was not fixed. He is sometimes tall, he is sometimes short, he is sometimes green, he is sometimes red, he is even blue or he is yellow. In the United States, the work of the writer Washington Owens and the poet Clemente Moore has further shaped the image of Santa Claus, but early illustrations still show him in a green or brown winter jacket. The British Fathers of Christmas and the Santas of the United States of America have come together to form the mixed version that we know today.

The green version of Santa Claus appeared not only in books and cards, but also in a reflection of people’s fear of nature and their desire for winter spring. Green represents life, hope and recovery and is perfectly compatible with the theme of Christmas celebrating the triumph of light over darkness. Many Victorian Christmas cards can see a sweet picture of the old man in green with a robin or a green plant.

How did the Coca-Cola commercial change Santa's color?

So, why is the green Santa being replaced by red? The most critical turning point occurred in 1931. At the time, Coca-Cola was facing the problem of poor sales of winter drinks and decided to promote the product through large-scale advertising. They hired the famous illustrator Haddon Sundblom to create a series of advertisements featuring Santa Claus.

Sambu portrays Santa Claus as a fat, smileful old man dressed in fresh red robes, white fur-side decorations, and a perfect match with Coca-Cola's brand of red. In the ad, Santa Claus was happy to drink Coca-Cola, and had a warm and happy breath. This series of ads has not only succeeded in promoting Coca-Cola. In winter, the red and white image of Santa Claus became more popular and spread rapidly across the globe.

Although Coca-Cola is not the first person to use Red Santa (the early Thomas Nast illustrations have begun to favour Red), their ads have been large and sustained for many years, making the red version mainstream. As the global influence of American culture grew during World War II, Red Santa almost completely replaced the early green image. Today, Santa Claus, which we see in malls, cards and movies, is almost all this red and white classic.

The meaning and modernity of the green Santa.

Although the mainstream image is already red, the green Santa is still in some cultures. In Brazil, for example, some drink brands have introduced green Santas to respond to national colours; in South Korea and elsewhere, there are also old Santas in greens. The green version reminds us that Christmas is not just a business and a gift, but a celebration of life, hope and sharing. Even in the cold winter, green is a symbol of never-ending life. Perhaps in the coming Christmas, we can reapply to the charm of the old man in green and make the tradition of Christmas more diverse. The next time you see a Christmas decoration, think of the warmth and hope brought by the compassionate old man who was once in a green robe, who still shines.
