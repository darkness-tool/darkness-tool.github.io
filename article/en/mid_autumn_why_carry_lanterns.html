Why do you raise the lanterns in mid-Autumn?

Author: Big Man Date of issue: 12 August 2026 Update: 12 August 2026 Reading time: About 8 minutes
HERO FIGURE

Why are the lights so moving in the middle of the fall? On August 15th, when the full moon rises, the streets and alleys will be lit up with a big, coloured lantern. Children with rabbit lamps, fish lamps or cartoon-type lanterns run in the night, while adults share their moon cakes and grapefruits with their families under the lights. This seemingly natural image actually carries more than a thousand years of culture. Why did you lift the lantern in mid-autum? Is it pure entertainment, or is it hidden in deeper history, faith and humanity? This post will be written from the historical literature, from popular legends to local differences, and will provide a complete picture of this tradition.

The Mid-Autumn Festival dates back to the ancient Emperor's ceremonial ceremonial ceremony, the earliest of which can be traced to the ceremonial ceremonial ceremonials of the Sun, Sun and Fall and Moon. When I arrived in Tang Dynasty, the moon became a human grace, and Song Dynasty became a festivities for all. The tradition of raising lanterns or burning them is an important part of the evolution of the tradition. The ancients thought that the Mid-Autumn Night, "the sky is as water and the moon is as bright as the sun" , but they still felt it was not enough, so they set up the tradition of "lights to help the moonlight" , with artificial lights and natural moonlights, to make it more a beautiful and pleasant holiday.

Historical Retrospective: From lighting tools to festival signs

The lantern itself is very early in origin and can be traced back to the time of Sihan, where it was initially a practical lighting device, covered with mackerel as a skeleton, paper or tatter, and contained candles or oil lamps to facilitate night walk and hang. When he arrived in Donghan, Hamingty advocated Buddhism and ordered the 15th of the month to "burn the lights" to be celebrated. However, the clear recording of the mid-autumn lights was the first in the region. According to the South China Sea Syndicate, at the end of the day, Kakai Iwaza, the South Han Times (from 7 to 971 A.D.) Guangzhou people have been praying in the capital and the Middle Autumn, known as "The Moon Lights". After the middle of the Qing Dynasty, the tradition of Zhongqiang or Zhongqiang was prevalent in the capital. The people made various kinds of fruit, birds and animals, fish and worm-shaped lanterns in the first ten days of the festival, and covered them with plasters. They lit them in mid-autumn and fall nights with ropes attached to the bamboo poles, high up on the roofs or on the terraces, and the rich were able to reach even a few of them, and the city was filled with light like a world of silk. This is not only a decorative act, but also a ritual of light for the Moon, and a prayer for harvest and peace.

Four core meanings: it's more than good-looking.

The first is to "light the road to a circle." The round shape of the lanterns responds to the full moon of the Mid-Autumn and the "round" in Chinese culture is complete and harmonious. The ancients used to put up lights or lights to light up the way home for their families and to place their hopes for early reunion with relatives from far away. Even though modern transport is convenient, this "light to the people" is still retained. The second level is Light and Hope. Lights are a sign of dispersing darkness and bringing light to the future. In mid-Autumn Day, people pray through lights for a good year and a happy home. In some areas, there are also river lights or skylights that further send the desire to the sky or water and pray for God's protection.

The third level is closely related to sound. The "light" is close to the "ding" so that the lantern is often blessed with "ting". The daughter who was married in the past will send a lantern in mid-autumn, hoping that she will keep her husband's house warm. This custom is particularly common in the Chinese communities in Hong Kong, Guangdong and overseas. The fourth level is festival and child education. When the lanterns turned from live lighting to toys and artworks, they became what the children most looked forward to. The traditional paper is made of rabbits, mackerel, omelet, etc., and modern times are used with plastics and LED lamps and even music. The children raised the lights and guessed them, not only to add joy, but also to understand the traditional culture in the future.

The difference: why is Taiwan different from South China?

It is worth noting that mid-autumn lanterns are not a common Chinese custom. In Taiwan, lanterns and charades are concentrated mainly on the Night's Festival, and in mid-Autumn they are dominated by barbecues, moon cakes, grapefruits and beauties. In contrast, in Hong Kong, Macau, Guangdong, Guangxi and Southeast Asian communities such as Singapore and Malaysia, the mid-autumn lantern is extremely prevalent. This is about the history of migration. The early Chinese from Guangdong and Fujian-South brought the tradition of Mid-Autumn to the South and integrated it with local culture, leading to the large-scale lighting and lighting of children and children seen today. In mid-Autumn, Viet Nam is considered "child's day" and a paper-candle parade is a central project. This geographical disparity reflects the resilience and vitality of Chinese culture in different environments.

From ancient times to the present: how the lantern became the symbol of Mid-Autumn

In recent times, mid-Autumn lights have been placed only after midnight, making them the second-largest traditional lights. In Chongzhou, there is also a unique tradition of "burning Watta" which has its origins in the flames used as a signal by the M.D. Han rebels, and which later becomes a sign of joy and a red fire. In addition, there is a tradition of lightships in Gangnam. Today, both hand-held paper lamps, plastic cartoons, and large theme lighting in the park continue to be used to "burn the moon." The parents take their children to the moon, both as parents and as a matter of cultural heritage. The lanterns are no longer just a lighting tool, but rather a bridge between the past and the present, between individuals and families, between people and the Moon.

In addition, the lanterns carry the faith of the people who pray for good and drive away evil. In some places, there is a legend that new lights in mid-Autumn can send away old bad luck and good luck; there are also those who believe that light fires can lead the way and make money for the gods of fortune. These seemingly superstitions reflect the deep-seated desire of the farming community for harvest, peace and the continuation of children. Even today, when we walk under the moon with a light cage, we can still feel the warmth and the hope of a time travel.

The story of mid-autumn lanterns is much richer than it is on the surface. It travels from light to light, through intergenerational changes, and integrates the ceremonial moons, prayers, reunions, additions and entertainments, and finally becomes the common cultural memory of the Chinese world. The next time you or your child talk about that lantern, think about it: this little light, the light of the night, is not just a small road, but a thousand years of Chinese pursuit of home, hope, life. May every lantern bring a light and a remix for your mid-fall.
