Surprised! The company's annual dinner will have to pay for itself? Netizens are angry: Is it reasonable?

Author: Alvin Issued: 1 January 2026 Update: 1 January 2026 Reading time: About 7 minutes

This is definitely killing hundreds of workers! For a whole year in the workplace, we all look forward to the company's annual dinner or tails, so that we can relax, have a big meal, get a gift and feel the comfort of the boss. However, a number of companies have recently exploded on the Internet, asking employees to participate at their own expense, even for AA share price. It makes a lot of employees look stupid, and it's like, "Does it have to pay for a year?" This situation has led to intense discussions among a wide range of Internet users, some who find it totally unreasonable and others who think it depends on the situation. This article is an exclusive compilation of real cases in the workplace, legal regulations and the voice of netizens, so that you can read the story of the "self-financed tail" and whether it really makes sense.

According to the Ministry of Labour, the tails or annual dinners are, by their nature, activities of company comfort workers and are not forced to work, so the company is not legally required to do so. However, if the company chooses to operate, it should normally be at the expense of the employer, as this is a benefit. A number of experts have pointed out that, while requiring staff members to pay for themselves, they are legal if they volunteer to participate, they can cause staff dissatisfaction and affect morale. Especially in times of economic stress, it is ironic that employees are already working for companies and are paying for their own "festivities." Internet users shared their experiences that some companies had insufficient budget and had been transferred to their employees, resulting in low attendance, which was counterproductive.

What does the employee say? The Internet is angry!

In the major forums and in the community, there is no shortage of post-posters from workers who are throwing up bitter water. One of the netizens said, "The company's tail is for $500, and I prefer to eat noodles at home!" And the other shared, "The festivities are so good to celebrate the results, and at the end of the day they say "AA" and everyone's face is green." Many people think that the annual dinner is an opportunity for the boss to thank the employees, and that if the employees are to pay, the welfare will be a burden. There are also employees who fear that non-attendance will be resented by their supervisors, affecting the grades or the end of the year. What is the logic of a company that does not make money when it does not pay for its activities? This voice reflects the impatience and dissatisfaction with the reality of the job, making the topic a hot spot quickly.

However, there was some disagreement. Some employees have indicated that if the tails are big, have a star show or a grand prize, it is acceptable to pay for it, especially if the company is clear in advance. But most people still find it unreasonable because the tradition of tailtooths is "boss buying." Legal experts add that it is already possible to invoke the law if companies force their participation without paying for overtime work or if they are disguised as requiring their own expenses. The employees suggested that, in such cases, they could discuss or consult the Labour Bureau in private to protect their rights. On the whole, this self-absorption has led many workers to rethink the real benefits of their jobs.

Why did the company do that? The reasons behind it are exposed.

Most of the reasons for the company's self-financing are inadequate budget or want to control costs. In particular, small and medium-sized enterprises prefer to reduce their benefits and not to award cash bonuses when their profits are uncertain. Some resource experts have analysed their tails to promote corporate image and outreach, but if full public expenditure is too high, they turn to employee distribution. There are also companies who believe that self-financing increases their willingness to attend, only to those who wish to participate. But this tends to ignore staff psychology and is counterproductive. In other cases, it has been shown that some companies charge fees for “voluntary participation” and that the non-attendance and non-refundance of employees has led to more controversy. The experts cautioned that companies should give priority to employees’ feelings if they want to do well, and that they would be better able to win by issuing vouchers or bonuses. This situation also reflects the multifaceted nature of Taiwan’s workplace benefits and gives thought to the balance between employers and employees.

In the face of this situation, many employees have opted for a low-profile response, with some not participating directly, and others in groups. Some companies have heard backslides and moved to full burden or cancelled activities. In the wake of the general debate, which led to the re-examination of the workplace culture, there was a debate about the real "comfort". Some netizens shared positive experiences: "We have a full tail and iPhone, and we are so happy!" Against this background, self-financing cases are more dramatic. In the future, perhaps more companies will reflect on how the annual dinner should be organized, so that employees will not be disappointed from their expectations, or even leave their will and ability to produce.
