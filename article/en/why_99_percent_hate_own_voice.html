Discovery! Why is 99% of the people embarrassed to delete the recording? Your voice has such a secret!

By Mina Date of issue: 24 November 2025 Update: 24 November 2025 Reading time: About 4 minutes

This is an event that has embarrassed millions of people! Do you have that experience? When you were excited to record the next message, or to listen to your own Podcast, all of a sudden, the voice that came out stopped you. "Is this really what I'm talking about? How can it sound so strange and unorthodox?” This feeling is not alone, and studies show that 99% of the people who first heard their own recording have a strong sense of rejection and even want to delete the file immediately. Why is that? Are we all born to be humbled by our voices? In fact, all of this is due to the ingenuity of the brain and the physical structure, so let's open up this sound puzzle together. Imagine, when you normally speak, the voices you hear are actually a mixture of "inline" and "outside" voices. But what's captured on the tape is the voice, which makes us strange. This phenomenon is not only widespread, but also relates to the scientific principles of bone and air transfer. Today we'll start from a scientific point of view and show you why your voice sounds so annoying. Ready? Let's get this sound on the adventure!

First, let's talk about the basic principles of voice transmission. When you speak, the sound waves not only pass through the air to the ears, but also through bones such as cheekbones and teeth, directly vibrating the inner ear. This bone transfer makes your voice sound lower and richer because the low-frequency waves are magnified. But when the tape recorder captures your voice, it records only the part of the air-transmitting, and it lacks the bone-transmitting 'hold'. As a result, you're heard by outsiders, and that's why you feel "not like yourself." In his TED speech, psychologist Rebecca Kleinberg pointed out that such a difference could trigger a brain alarm system that would innately exclude it. Like when we look in the mirror, we get used to the mirror ourselves, but if we suddenly see a real image, we feel uncomfortable. This "sound out of tune" phenomenon, ubiquitous in everyday life, from phone calls to self-censorship in videos, can cause similar embarrassment. Interestingly, research has found that women are more likely to be negative about their voices because of social stereotypes of voices. For example, too high a voice may be considered unprofessional, too low and too arrogant, adding to internal conflicts.

What do scientists say?

In the scientific field, a survey from the Voice shows that about 80% of the people do not like their voices, with a higher percentage of women. According to experts, this is not only a biological factor but also a mechanism for the sound management of the brain. When we hear other people's voices, the brain automatically filters background noise and focuses on content; but when we listen to our own voices, we overanalyze every detail, moving from sound to speed. This "excessive self-push" makes the small points more problematic, such as "my voice is too nasal" or "it sounds so false." In addition, childhood experience plays a key role. Many remember being ridiculed by their classmates when they were little and left a shadow. In Japan, a study found that women tend to speak loudly in order to meet social expectations, but that instead makes them more vocal in their recording. TED speaker Rebecca Kleinberg further explained that there are three types of voice experience: inner voice (imagining in the brain), internal voice (hearing when speaking) and external voice (recording). The difference between the three is precisely the cause of 99 per cent of the people who hate their voices. She suggested that listening to her own recording could adapt to the difference as if she were used to new hair. The scientific evidence is that the continuous exposure of the sensory path that recreates the brain allows you to slowly fall in love with the "real self." So, next time you record, don't rush, give yourself a chance to adapt!

In addition to physical reasons, psychological factors are also indispensable. Many people link their voices to their own image, and feel that it represents personality or attraction. The rise of self-censorship in social media is compounded by this dissatisfaction. "My voice sounds like cartoon characters. It's so naive." But in fact, it's just a protection mechanism for the brain, so we don't depend too much on outsiders. Interestingly, celebrities such as Emma Stone have publicly admitted that they hate their voices, proving that this is a general reality. To overcome it, start with a little exercise: take a diary every day and gradually accept that unique voice. Gradually, you'll find it's actually charming. And remember, everyone's voice is unique and worthy of appreciation.

Reader Sharing and Hot

On social platforms, the topic has been the subject of intense discussion. "Every time you listen to your voice, it's like an alien invader." Another adds: "It's a bone-drive pot, and it's released immediately after the conference." Many share their experience in overcoming it, others join the singing community and regain their self-confidence through training, and some use the App to adjust the audio recording to simulate the sound inside. The expert suggests that the focus should not be just on the gaps, but on the merits, such as the unique sound or rhythm. The community's discussion goes beyond complaining and extends to the social impact of voices: why are women's voices often criticized? This has led to reflection on gender stereotypes and calls for greater inclusion. Some of the readers proposed a "sound-in workshop" to record each other and give them a positive response. Such actions not only heal individuals, but also increase human connections. A mother shared: "Teaching children to love their own voice and avoiding ridicule from childhood can break the cycle." In the heat, there was a consensus that understanding the truth was the first step, followed by brave embraces. This discussion has freed hundreds of people from the embarrassment to celebrate the diversity of their voices. And you? Ready to hear your voice again?

Now, as technology advances, many tools can help us adapt to our voices. Like Speechify App, you can immediately switch the tone and bring the recording closer to the inside. But experts remind me that real change comes from within: acceptance of imperfections is the key to confidence. Rethinking the moments that hate voices is actually an opportunity to grow up and let us know ourselves better. In the workplace, a clear voice is an advantage and practice enhances communication. Many successful people, such as speakers, started with hateful voices and turned into super-powerful weapons. Community residents started organizing online sharing sessions to encourage each other to record the challenge. This not only increases self-identification, but also strengthens emotional ties. One of the participants said, "Now, I love my voice, it sounds full of stories. The change makes life easier. In the end, it reminds us that the brain is stubborn, but habits change everything.

How do we improve?

To get rid of the shackles of voices? First, step-by-step exposure: one minute a day, from short to long, gradually adapting. Second, the bone transferor: it simulates the inner voice and makes the recording more relevant. Thirdly, sound training: Toastmasters or speech courses to enhance self-confidence. Fourth, positive: list the strengths of the sound, such as warmness or unique taste. (e) Fifth, assistance to a professional: a psychologist or a vocal teacher if the mood is seriously affected. Remember, 99% of people have experienced it, but only 1% have chosen to change. Which side do you want? These little steps can turn you from embarrassment to pride. Finally, hug your voice, it's your most loyal partner!
